## Actual Answers Provided By Selah

*"Church members are about to lose the matriarch of the family. Show me a few honest, comforting things I could say that don't rush past the grief."*

This is a sacred and hard moment to step into. A few things worth saying — slowly, without agenda:

"We love her. And we're going to miss her terribly."

Simple, true, and it names the loss rather than explaining it away. Sometimes the most comforting thing is just someone else saying out loud what everyone is feeling but holding in.

"She has shaped this community in ways we're still living in — and will be for a long time."

Matriarchs form people. The way she prayed, how she welcomed, what she insisted on — it's woven into others. This honors the reality that her life has already done its work, and that work doesn't vanish with her.

"We don't have to rush this. Grief is allowed to take the space it needs."

Most grieving people feel quiet pressure to be okay sooner than they are. Naming that the community will hold space — and isn't tracking a timeline — is a real gift.

"You don't have to carry this alone. That's what we're here for."

Concrete and communal. Not "call me if you need anything" (which puts the burden on the grieving) — but a statement that presence is already assumed.

"It's okay to feel all of it — the gratitude and the grief at the same time."

Loss of a long, full life often comes with complicated feelings: relief that suffering is ending, grief at the absence, gratitude for the years, and sadness all at once. Giving permission for that complexity matters.

One thing worth not saying — even though it feels comforting — is anything that jumps too quickly to resolution: "She's in a better place," "At least she lived a full life," "God needed another angel." These may be true or well-intentioned, but said too soon they can feel like someone changing the subject on the grief. Let the loss be named first. The hope can come in its time.

*Is this for a specific moment — a bedside conversation, a pastoral visit, a eulogy, something else? The right words shift depending on where you're standing.*

*"I'm about to visit someone in the hospital who's facing routine surgery, but is expecting the worst. Give me a short, honest response to their situation that I can say that doesn't feel like a script."*

This is a pastoral request, not a theological exploration — so I'm going to step outside the Selah format entirely and just answer you directly.

The most honest thing you can say to someone who's catastrophizing before routine surgery isn't reassurance — it's presence. Something like:

"I don't know how this goes. But I'm here, and I'm not going anywhere."

Or if it fits your relationship and their faith:

"You're scared, and that makes sense. You don't have to pretend otherwise with me."

What tends to fall flat: "It'll be fine" (they don't believe you), "the doctors are great" (not the point), or anything that tries to talk them out of their fear. Fear doesn't need to be argued with — it needs to be witnessed.

If they have faith and would receive it, you could ask if you can pray with them before you leave. Not a long prayer. Just something short and honest — even something like "God, she's scared. Be close." That kind of prayer names reality without pretending to fix it.

*The fact that you're going at all is already most of the thing.*
