*"Kids sometimes ask hard questions like why did God let my grandma die? Give me a short, honest, age-appropriate way to answer that doesn't dodge or overcomplicate it. And show me a list of other such questions with answers. I want to be prepared."*

This is a practical request, not a theological exploration session — so I'll step outside the Micro-Loop format and give you something directly useful.

### "Why Did God Let Grandma Die?"

For ages roughly 5–10:

> "That's one of the hardest questions there is, and it's okay that it hurts to ask it. Here's what we know: God made us to live, and death wasn't his first idea — something went wrong in the world a long time ago, and now everyone dies. God didn't forget Grandma. He loves her more than we do. And we get to be sad about missing her, because that's real. We don't always get to know exactly why things happen when they do. But we know God was with her, and he's with us right now too."

What this does: it's honest about not knowing the when, doesn't pretend God caused it or had nothing to do with it, and holds grief as legitimate rather than something to fix.

### Other Questions Kids Ask — with Honest, Age-Appropriate Answers

**"Why doesn't God just stop bad things from happening?"**

> "He could stop everything — but he made a world where people get to make real choices, and sometimes people choose things that hurt others. And sometimes hard things just happen because the world is broken. God isn't sitting far away not caring. He came into the broken world himself, in Jesus, so he knows what it feels like from the inside."

**"If God is real, why can't I see him?"**

> "You can't see the wind either, but you can feel it and see what it does. God is real in a different way than chairs and trees are real. He's not hiding — he's just bigger than what our eyes can hold. Some people say they feel him most when they're not alone even though they're by themselves."

**"Does God get mad at me?"**

> "God sees everything you do, including the stuff you'd rather he didn't. But here's the thing — he already knew all of that and decided to love you anyway. He cares when you do something wrong the way a good parent does: not to crush you, but because it matters. He's not waiting to catch you. He's hoping you'll come back."

**"What happens when we die?"**

> "We don't know everything, and anyone who says they do is guessing. What we do know is what Jesus said: that he was going to make a place for the people he loves, and that he'd bring them there. Death isn't the end — but what's on the other side is bigger than our words can really hold. It's okay to not totally understand it."

**"Why did God make the devil?"**

> "God didn't make a devil — he made an angel who chose to turn away from him. That choice was real, and the consequences were real. It's a strange and dark story, but it means evil isn't something God invented. It's what happens when something good turns away from the source of good."

**"Does God love everyone, even really bad people?"**

> "Yes. That's one of the hardest things about God — he loves people we might not think deserve it. That doesn't mean what bad people do is okay. It means love isn't the same as approval. He loves them and still hates what they do. He loves you the same way — all of you, not just your best moments."

**"Why do I have to pray if God already knows what I need?"**

> "God doesn't need the information — you're right about that. But prayer isn't really a report. It's more like talking to someone you love. You don't tell your parents things because they don't know — you tell them because you're close. Prayer is practice at being close."

**One principle underneath all of these:**

Don't say "I don't know" and stop there — that feels like abandonment to a kid. But also don't fill the not-knowing with false certainty. The move is: name what we don't know, then anchor to what we do. Kids can hold mystery better than we think, as long as they're not holding it alone.
