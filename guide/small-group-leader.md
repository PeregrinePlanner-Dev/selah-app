# Small Group Leader

## Leading the Discussion

* "Sunday's sermon was on [topic/passage]. Give me 4-5 discussion questions that go deeper than just recapping the sermon. Provide real-world applications the group can use during this next week."
* "We're studying [book/topic] this season. Give me a simple outline for this week's session — a few key points and questions to open it up."
* "Give me an icebreaker tied to [topic] that doesn't feel forced."

## Following Up & Care

* "Someone in my group mentioned [situation] last week. Give me a few thoughtful things I could say when I check in with them this week."
* "I want to send a short text checking on the group after a heavy discussion on [topic]. Help me word it so it feels caring, not like a formality."

## Welcoming New People

* "A new person is joining our group this week. Give me a couple of easy ways to include them without putting them on the spot."

## Handling Group Dynamics

* "One person tends to dominate our discussions. Give me a few gentle ways to open the floor to others without singling anyone out."

## Multiplying & Training New Leaders

* "I want to train an apprentice leader to eventually take over or start a new group. Give me a simple plan for what to hand off and when."
* "Our group has grown past what feels like a good discussion size. Help me think through how to split it into two groups without it feeling like a breakup."

## Actual Answers Provided By Selah

*"Sunday's sermon was on watching for end times signals. Give me 4-5 discussion questions that go deeper than just recapping the sermon. Provide real-world applications the group can use during this next week."*

These are good to work with — though I want to flag one thing before offering them.

The request is for discussion questions and applications, which I can do. But the questions below are built to open the theological territory, not just recap a sermon — which means some of them will surface real disagreements and unresolved tensions. That's intentional. A small group that only confirms what the sermon said hasn't really gone deeper; it's just repeated it with more people.

So these are written to generate genuine conversation, not consensus. Use the ones that fit your group's readiness.

1. **What are we actually watching for — and why?** Jesus said "watch" (Matt 24:42; Mark 13:33) — but he also said no one knows the day or hour, not even the Son (Matt 24:36). So what does faithful watching look like if it can't be calendar-checking? Is there a difference between alert, expectant living and trying to decode a timetable? Where does your group draw that line?

2. **Who was Jesus talking to?** Read Matthew 24:1–3 together. The disciples ask about the destruction of the temple and the end of the age in the same breath. Many of Jesus's warnings in that chapter were fulfilled with terrifying precision in AD 70, when Rome destroyed Jerusalem. Does it change how you read the "signs" if some of them already happened? How do you decide which parts are past, which are future, and which might be both?

3. **The sign-reading problem.** Every generation since the first century has had people convinced they were living in the final days — Napoleon, WWI, WWII, the Cold War, 9/11. They weren't wrong to take Scripture seriously. But they were wrong about the timeline. What does that track record tell us about how confident we should be in current sign-reading? What's the difference between taking Jesus's words seriously and claiming to know more than he said we could know?

4. **What does your eschatology make you do?** This is the application question. Two broad postures show up in church history: one says the world is burning anyway, so invest in souls, not structures — the other says God is renewing creation, so what we build, restore, and care for now participates in that future. Neither is obviously wrong. But they lead to very different choices about how you spend your time, money, and energy this week. Which posture shapes your actual life right now — and is that the one you'd defend on reflection?

5. **Hope as ethics.** The early church lived in genuine expectation of Christ's imminent return. That expectation produced a particular way of life — radical generosity, indifference to status, unusual care for the poor and vulnerable. If you genuinely believed Christ was returning soon, what would you do differently starting Monday? And if the answer is "nothing," what does that tell you about how deep the belief actually runs?

Real-world applications this week:

* Monday: Identify one thing you've been postponing because it feels too long-term or uncertain. Ask whether "watching for the end" should make you less or more invested in it.
* Mid-week: Read Matthew 24:36–44 slowly, and sit with the tension — genuine urgency plus genuine ignorance of timing. Try to hold both without resolving them too quickly.
* Ongoing: Notice where end-times language shows up in your news feed, social media, or conversation. Practice the question: is this faithful watchfulness or something else?

One closing note: if the sermon took a specific position on the millennium or rapture timing, some of these questions will implicitly push back on it — not to be difficult, but because these are the genuinely contested questions. Your group can decide how far to go. Questions 1 and 5 are the safest starting points; 2 and 3 are where the real theological friction is.

*"I want to train an apprentice leader to eventually take over or start a new group. Give me a simple plan for what to hand off and when. I am reminded of Paul working with his disciples. Any basis there I can draw on?"*

There's real substance in the Paul-Timothy-Titus pattern worth drawing out before we get to a handoff plan — because the how of what Paul did shapes what you'd actually hand off and when.

Paul's approach wasn't a curriculum transfer. It was life-on-life with increasing responsibility. The clearest window into it is 2 Timothy 2:2:

> "The things which you have heard from me in the presence of many witnesses, entrust these to faithful men who will be able to teach others also." (2 Timothy 2:2, NASB)

Notice the four-generation chain embedded in one verse: Paul → Timothy → faithful men → others. Paul isn't thinking about one succession — he's thinking about a reproducible pattern. What gets handed off is not just content but the capacity to hand it off again.

The other key text is what Paul says in 1 Corinthians about his own modeling posture:

> "Be imitators of me, just as I also am of Christ." (1 Corinthians 11:1, NASB)

That's a bold thing to say — and it points to something the Paul-Timothy relationship makes concrete: apprenticeship works when the apprentice can watch the leader lead, not just hear them teach. Timothy traveled with Paul. He saw how Paul handled conflict (Acts 15), how he responded to suffering (2 Timothy 3:10–11), how he related to churches he'd planted.

So here's a simple framework drawn from that pattern — three phases, each with a different center of gravity:

### Phase 1 — Alongside (Observe and Assist)

The apprentice is with you. They watch how you make decisions, handle difficult people, prepare, debrief. They're not leading yet — they're learning the texture of leadership before they hold the title. You debrief everything. The goal: they can articulate why you do what you do, not just what you do.

### Phase 2 — Under Supervision (Lead with Oversight)

You hand them specific, bounded responsibilities — lead this session, handle this conversation, make this call. You debrief after. You let them fail in low-stakes situations and help them name what happened. The goal: they develop their own leadership instincts, not just an imitation of yours.

### Phase 3 — Commissioned (Released with Connection)

They lead. You're available but not present. The Paul-Timothy letters are worth noting here: Paul wasn't hovering — he was in prison — but he was still writing, still available for counsel, still invested. Release doesn't mean disappearance. The goal: they're genuinely leading, and you're genuinely their resource, not their supervisor.

What gets handed off across those phases, in rough order: your practices first (how you prepare, how you open, how you close), then your relationships (introducing them to key people, letting trust transfer), then your judgment calls (the harder stuff — how you handle a struggling member, a conflict, a theological question you don't have a clean answer to), and finally your authority (the explicit commissioning, public if possible).

*Here's a question worth sitting with before you build this out further: what's your honest read on where your apprentice is right now? Are they in Phase 1 territory — still learning the what of what you do — or have they already been watching long enough that they're ready for Phase 2 responsibility? That shapes everything about what to hand off first.*
