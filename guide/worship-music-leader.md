# Worship & Music Leader

## Building the Set List

* "Sunday's message is on [topic/passage]. Give me a few song themes or ideas that would actually connect with it, not just loosely relate."
* "I need a set that moves from [mood/theme — e.g. lament to hope]. Give me a simple structure for how the songs could build toward that."

## Vetting Lyrics & Theology

* "Here are the lyrics to [song]. Is there anything theologically off or worth being careful about before we use it?"
* "Give me a plain-language explanation of what [song] is actually saying theologically, so I can decide if it fits our church."

## Talking to the Team

* "I want to explain to the team why we're choosing [song/theme] this week — give me a short, clear reason I can share before rehearsal."

## Special Services & Themed Sundays

* "We're planning a [Christmas Eve/Good Friday/baptism Sunday] service. Give me a few song ideas that would fit the tone. Something with a new perspective would be nice. We seem to do the same things every year."

## Shepherding the Team

* "I want to open rehearsal with something more than announcements. Give me a short devotional thought tied to [song/theme] we're working on this week."
* "One of my team members seems burned out. Give me a few honest, caring things I could say to check in without making it awkward."

## Explaining a Style or Direction Change

* "We're shifting toward [more contemporary/more traditional] worship and I know it won't land well with everyone. Give me a clear, respectful way to explain why, without dismissing what people are attached to."
* "Someone told me they don't feel like they can worship anymore since we changed the music style. Give me a caring way to respond that takes their experience seriously."

## Actual Answers Provided By Selah

*"I want to open worship team rehearsal with something more than announcements. Give me a short devotional thought tied to building faith through music that we're working on this week."*

This is a practical request for worship leadership — not a theology exploration session. I can offer something useful here, but I want to be straightforward: Selah is built for personal theological exploration, so what I give you will be a devotional thought in that spirit rather than a polished liturgical script.

Here's one angle worth sitting with before your team picks up their instruments:

Music does something words alone can't — it carries truth into the body. You feel a chord resolve before you can explain why it matters. Rhythm gets inside you. And this means worship musicians are doing something unusual: you're not just communicating about faith, you're practicing it in real time, together, in front of people.

Hebrews 11:1 says faith is "the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not yet seen." That's the territory music lives in — sound is invisible, hope is invisible, but both are real and both do something in us.

The question worth raising with your team before you start: are we performing this music, or are we trusting through it? There's a difference. Performance is execution — you nail the part, you hit the note, you feel good about it. Trust is different — it's the moment when the music is doing something in you and through you that you didn't produce, and you know it.

That's the mode worth finding before Sunday.

*If your team is working through a specific song or theme this week, I'm happy to pull a passage or a tension from the theology of that theme that might give the devotional more traction.*

*"We're shifting toward more contemporary worship and I know it won't land well with everyone. Give me a clear, respectful, bible-based way to explain why, without dismissing what people are attached to."*

This is a real tension, and the fact that you're thinking about how to explain it well — rather than just announcing it — already says something.

Here's a frame worth sitting with before you build your explanation:

The deepest question underneath worship style debates isn't usually what we do in worship, but who worship is for. And Scripture holds two things in tension that are genuinely hard to hold together.

On one side: worship is for God. It's directed toward him, and the content — the truths being declared about his character, his acts, his worth — matters. Psalm 95 opens with an exuberant call ("come, let us sing for joy to the Lord") and moves straight into why: "for the Lord is the great God, a great King above all gods" (vv. 1–3). The emotional pitch of worship follows from the theological content. That's not style — that's substance.

On the other side: worship has always been embodied in a particular time, place, and people. The Psalms use instruments and forms that were contemporary to their culture. The early church adapted. Every hymn that now feels ancient was once new to someone.

So here's the tension worth naming honestly with your congregation:

Form serves truth — it doesn't replace it. The question isn't whether contemporary or traditional worship is more "spiritual." It's whether the form you're moving toward carries the weight of what you're trying to declare about God. That's a real question, worth asking seriously, and it invites the people who are grieving the change to participate in the answer — not just accept a decision.

That framing does a few things: it honors what people love (the content and reverence they associate with traditional worship) without pretending it's the only faithful form. It names the actual stakes. And it gives the congregation a theological question to wrestle with together, rather than a consumer preference to argue over.

*What's driving the shift for you — is it primarily a reaching-outward impulse (evangelism, accessibility), or something about where your congregation itself is spiritually right now?*

*"The staff feels like we are stuck in the 60's. We want to be sensitive to the younger audience that is resonating with the pastor's preaching style."*

That's a clarifying answer — and it surfaces a distinction worth being honest about internally before you build your public explanation.

There are actually two different rationales sitting inside what you just described, and they pull in slightly different directions:

One is missional — reaching people who aren't yet in the room, or who are on the edge. Younger people who resonate with the preaching but feel the disconnect between what they hear from the pulpit and what they experience in the worship set. That's a legitimate concern rooted in something like Paul's "become all things to all people" instinct (1 Corinthians 9:22).

The other is internal — the staff feels stuck. That's a real feeling, and it may be completely valid. But it's a different kind of reason, and if it leaks into your explanation to the congregation, it will land as "we're bored with what you love" — which is not what you mean, but it's what people will hear.

The reason this matters: people who are attached to the current form aren't just attached to a style. They're attached to a set of memories, a community that formed around those forms, a sense that this is how we meet God here. Dismissing that — even accidentally — doesn't just lose the argument. It communicates that their experience of worship wasn't real or valuable. That's worth protecting against.

So the honest, pastoral move may be to lead with the missional reason — because it's the one that invites people into something larger than preference. "We want to reach people who are already responding to what God is doing through our preaching" is a reason that asks the congregation to give something up for others, which is a move Scripture endorses. That's a harder ask, but a more honorable one than "it's time for a change."

*What does your congregation know about who you're trying to reach? Is the missional vision already shared language, or would that framing itself be new to them?*
