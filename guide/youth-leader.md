## Actual Answers Provided By Selah

*"I need honest ideas to open tonight's lesson on witnessing to their sphere of influence in a natural way that would actually grab a room of teenagers, not adults?"*

What's the crowd like — are these teens who grew up in church and have heard "share your faith" a hundred times, or is it more mixed?

That changes the opening a lot. A room full of church kids who are quietly immune to the topic needs a different crack in the door than a room that's genuinely new to the question.

Either way, here's one idea worth considering:

**Open with a moment of social awkwardness, not a Bible verse.**

Ask them to think of the last time a friend told them something — a show, a song, a restaurant — and they didn't tell anyone else about it. Then ask: why not? Usually because they didn't actually care that much. Then flip it: think of the last time something genuinely got to them and they couldn't stop talking about it.

Don't name Jesus yet. Just sit in that for a second.

The question underneath is: if you're not talking about your faith, what does that tell you about how real it feels to you right now? — but you don't say that out loud. You let them get there.

That's not shame. It's a mirror. Teenagers can smell a lecture coming from three sentences away and they'll check out. But they'll lean in to a question about themselves.

*What's the actual tension you want them to leave with tonight? That'll shape what I'd suggest for the rest of the arc.*

*"Rework that idea, but make it more impactful."*

Here's a sharper version:

**Walk in with your phone out. Don't explain why.**

Pull up your most-played song, your most-watched YouTube channel, or your most-used app — whatever it is — and just show them. No setup. Then ask three or four students to do the same. Let it get a little awkward and funny.

Then say this:

"Your phone knows what you actually care about. Not what you say you care about — what you actually care about."

Pause. Let that land.

Then: "When's the last time you recommended any of those things to someone?"

They'll all have stories. The show they made their friend watch. The song they sent at midnight. The game they wouldn't shut up about.

Then the turn:

"Nobody had to teach you to do that. Nobody gave you a three-step method. You just... couldn't help it."

One more pause.

"So here's the only question for tonight: What does it mean if you've never once felt that way about Jesus?"

Don't answer it. Don't soften it. Don't immediately go to encouragement.

Just let it sit in the room.

That question is the lesson. Everything after it — Scripture, discussion, application — is unpacking what just happened in their chest when they heard it.

*"I need to explain why we're talking to the youth group about the suicide of one of the high school kids this week. There's a parent who will be uneasy about it. Give me a few clear, respectful bible-based talking points."*

This is a hard week. I'm glad you're thinking carefully about how to handle it — both for the kids and for the parent who's uneasy.

Here are a few grounded, pastoral talking points you can adapt:

1. **We talk about this because silence does harm.** Scripture is honest about human suffering in ways that can surprise us. The Psalms are full of anguish — including Psalm 88, which ends without resolution, without a tidy "but God is good" turn. It's in the canon. God is not afraid of the darkest human experiences, and neither should his church be. For teenagers who are grieving and possibly struggling themselves, a church that goes silent sends a message: bring that somewhere else. That's the opposite of what we want.

2. **We are not explaining or justifying — we are grieving together.** The goal isn't to answer every theological question about what happened. It's to do what Romans 12:15 calls us to: "Weep with those who weep." That's it. These kids lost someone. The church's job this week is presence, not doctrine.

3. **We will speak with care, not with clinical detail.** There are real guidelines (often called "safe messaging") about how to discuss suicide with young people — specifically, avoiding graphic detail or framing it as a solution to pain. You can reassure this parent that you're following those principles. The goal is to open space for grieving students to talk to a trusted adult, not to give anyone a roadmap.

4. **We hold the theological questions with humility.** If the parent's unease is partly theological — what does the church say about someone who died this way? — the honest answer is that the church's historical harshness on this question has caused real harm, and that most careful pastoral theologians today distinguish between the moral weight of an act and God's judgment of a person. We are not in a position to make that judgment. What we can say is that God's character is one of mercy, that Christ entered the depths of human suffering, and that the ground of anyone's salvation is grace, not the circumstances of their death.

5. **We are protecting the living.** Research is clear that grief communities — especially teenagers — are at elevated risk in the weeks after a suicide. The reason to address this directly, pastorally, and carefully is to reduce that risk. Talking about it in a safe, structured setting is protective. Silence is not.

If it helps to have one anchor verse for the conversation with the parent, Psalm 34:18 is simple and true: "The Lord is close to the brokenhearted and saves those who are crushed in spirit." That's the posture of this week.
